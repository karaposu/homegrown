# Thinking-Space Traversal, and Three Ways to Picture It

## What thinking-space traversal is

Imagine you're trying to understand something hard — not look up a fact, but actually work your way toward an idea that fits. You don't know in advance where that idea is. You have a rough sense of the problem, a vast field of possible directions you could think in, and somewhere in that field are the few ideas that would actually resolve things. Getting from the problem to one of those ideas is the whole game. That movement — from "I have a question" through a space of possibilities toward "I have an understanding that fits" — is what we call **thinking-space traversal**.

The word *traversal* is doing real work. You're not teleporting to the answer and you're not deducing it in one clean step. You're moving: trying a direction, seeing whether it pays off, following it further or giving up on it, and carrying what you learn forward. It looks less like solving an equation and more like foraging.

A few things are true of any honest version of this, and they're worth naming plainly, because the three pictures below are each vivid about a different one.

**You search without a map.** At the start you genuinely don't know which direction holds the good idea. So you have to pick one and go, on partial evidence, and stay willing to be wrong. When a direction starts paying off — the thinking feels like it's fitting the problem — you lean into it and go deeper. When a direction dead-ends, the smart move isn't to keep grinding; it's to back up to an earlier fork, a place where you could have gone another way, and try that branch instead. Explore, commit to what's working, back up when stuck. That's the *movement* itself.

**You have to remember where you've been.** A search that forgets is doomed to wander the same ground forever. So a good traversal keeps a record — of what it has learned, of which directions turned out rich, and, just as importantly, of which ground it has already covered so it doesn't waste effort re-treading it. Over many separate sessions of thinking, that record is the thing that lets understanding *accumulate* instead of resetting to zero each time.

**You have to spend limited attention wisely.** You can't chase every promising direction at once. Attention, effort, focus — these are a shared, finite pool, and traversal means constantly deciding where to pour more and where to pull back. A direction that's paying off earns more; a direction that's died has its share reclaimed and redirected somewhere with more promise.

**And no single viewpoint sees the whole thing.** This is the subtle one. Real understanding of anything large isn't held complete in one head at one moment. It's approached across many partial passes — many separate attempts, none of which sees everything — held together by the shared record they all read from and write to. The record is what makes the many partial searches add up to something none of them contained alone.

Underneath all of it is a surprisingly simple engine: **search without knowing, reinforce what pays, prune what doesn't, and keep a record so the next pass starts ahead of the last.** That's the pattern. Now here are three ways to see it.

## Three ways to picture it

### 1. A fungus foraging underground

Picture the pale, threadlike network a fungus grows through soil — the mycelium. It's hunting for food, and it has no idea where the food is. So it pushes threads outward in many directions at once. Where a thread runs into a rich patch of nutrients, the fungus thickens it and branches hard into that area. Where a thread finds nothing, it's abandoned, and — this is the lovely part — its material is pulled back and reused to fund growth elsewhere. The whole network shares one pool of resources: a good patch found on one side feeds new growth on the other. And the fungus has no brain anywhere in it. There's no central manager deciding all this; the pattern emerges from a lot of local pushing and flowing.

**How it maps.** The soil is the thinking space; the nutrient patches are the ideas that actually fit the problem. Pushing a thread into new ground is *trying a direction*. Thickening toward a rich patch is *leaning into what's paying off*. Abandoning a starved thread and pushing out instead from an older junction is exactly *backing up to an earlier fork when you're stuck*. And here's the thing this picture is best at: the network the fungus grows **is its memory**. The body you end up with is a physical record of everywhere you searched and everywhere you found something. It doesn't keep notes about its search — the search and the record are the same object. That's the picture to hold when you want to feel how understanding *accumulates and stays*: you grow a structure, and the structure is what you've learned.

### 2. A slime mold flowing across a surface

A slime mold — Physarum is the famous one — is stranger and, in one respect, sharper. It's a single creature, but instead of growing and staying put, it *flows*. It can pour its whole body toward food and abandon a spot entirely, picking up and relocating in a way a rooted fungus never does. As it moves, it forms a web of tubes, and it constantly thickens the tubes carrying the most traffic while letting the idle ones wither away — so it's forever tuning itself toward efficient routes. (In a famous experiment, when food was laid out at the positions of a city's train stations, the slime mold grew a network of tubes that closely matched the real rail map — a brainless blob rediscovering a design human engineers had labored over.) And it does one more thing worth dwelling on: it leaves a trail of slime everywhere it has already been, and it *avoids* that trail. The slime is an external note-to-self — "already looked here, don't bother again."

**How it maps.** The flowing-toward-food is the search-move again, but with a power the fungus lacks: the slime mold can pull its whole investment out of a region and re-commit somewhere else — it *re-decides*, rather than only extending. Thickening the busy tubes and starving the idle ones is spending limited attention on what's paying and reclaiming it from what isn't. But the standout is that slime trail. It's a record kept **outside** the living body — separate from the active search, laid down in the world, and read back later specifically to *avoid repeating yourself*. That maps cleanly onto keeping written notes: the point of writing down where you've already searched is so that later — a different day, a fresh start — you don't grind back over settled ground. This is the picture to hold when you want to feel two things the fungus blurs together: a search that can genuinely change its mind, and a memory that lives outside the search so it doesn't have to be re-derived each time.

### 3. Humanity working out its knowledge over centuries

Step all the way back. No single person figured out modern medicine, or physics, or how the climate works. Those understandings were reached by an enormous number of minds, each of them mortal, working across centuries, most of them never meeting. What connects them — what lets a thought from someone four hundred years dead reach the person building on it today — is the written record. A researcher reads what others left behind, adds one careful piece, writes it down, and eventually dies; the record carries their piece forward to the next person, who does the same. Fields pour effort into questions that are yielding results and quietly abandon lines that lead nowhere. And crucially: this actually *works*. We really did cure diseases and explain the tides. So this isn't only a nice picture of how understanding *might* accumulate — it's standing proof that the whole arrangement produces real understanding.

**How it maps.** Each individual mind is one pass through the thinking space — partial, limited, and mortal; nobody holds the whole thing. The shared literature is the record that outlives any single searcher, the thing that makes the many partial passes add up. Whole disciplines reinforcing fruitful directions and dropping dead ones is the reinforce-and-prune engine running at the scale of a civilization. This is the picture to hold for the two things the small-scale pictures can't show: first, the plain proof that many mortal, partial searches bridged by a durable record genuinely *arrive* at understanding — it's not a hope, it's history. And second, an honest note the tidier pictures gloss over — the individual searchers are finite and, in a sense, disposable; it's the *record* that persists and carries the work, not any one of the minds that contributed to it.

## Why three, and not one

You might reasonably ask why we keep three pictures instead of picking the best. It's because each one is vivid about a different part of the same simple engine, and no single picture carries all of them without going blurry.

The fungus is the clearest picture of a **record that accumulates** — you grow a body, and the body is what you've learned, kept and added to. The slime mold is the clearest picture of a search that **re-decides** and of a **memory kept outside** the search so you don't repeat yourself. And humanity is the clearest picture of the **whole thing actually working** across many finite, partial minds — the proof, and the honest reminder that the record outlasts the searchers.

Hold all three loosely and together, and you have a rounded feel for what thinking-space traversal is: not a leap to the answer, but a long foraging walk through a space you can't see the far side of — pushing into the dark, leaning into what pays, backing up when it doesn't, and leaving a good enough trail that the next walk, whoever takes it, starts further along than the last.
