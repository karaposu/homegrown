## Finding-Ready Pieces

### Q1 — Do I understand, and is the user right? Yes — on the architecture's own terms.
Yes. And not just as agreement — the canon's own layer model says so. "What should I work on next?" is a cross-inquiry selection, and the SUSTRALL architecture assigns that to the orchestrator (seen-for by the navigational session), both *above* the inquiry boundary the worker loop lives below. Five canon-grounded reasons converge (Q2). So this is confirmed on structural grounds, not conceded.

### Q2 — Why is next-move-selection navigation/orchestrator, not the worker loop? [CENTER]
The canon's unit of operation runs *probe → see → decide → dispatch*: a **worker** runs one inquiry to a finding (**probe**); the **navigational session** enumerates the field over the finished work (**see**); the **orchestrator** selects the next route (**decide**); the next session starts (**dispatch**). "What should I work on next?" is the **decide/select** step — it happens *after* a worker's probe, one layer up. Five reasons make it structurally not the worker's:

1. **Whole-field visibility.** Selecting requires seeing the whole field of directions. A worker is heads-down in one inquiry with one topic and can't see it; the navigational session is deliberately fresh + isolated + warmed precisely *so it can*.
2. **Control scope (canon-defined).** The worker runner's control scope is *per-cycle only* — conclude-or-iterate within its own inquiry. Cross-inquiry selection is the orchestrator's move. This is the architecture's stated allocation.
3. **Sees vs chooses.** Even the layer that sees the whole field is barred from choosing — *"Navigation sees; it does not choose."* Choosing is the orchestrator's. Both are above the worker; the worker is two roles removed from selection.
4. **Freshness / anti-tunnel-vision.** The navigational session is a *fresh session, fresh perception each time* — specifically to keep a deep worker's narrow, biased view out of the next-move decision.
5. **Dispatch order.** By the time a worker runs, "what next" was *already answered* — that's *why* it has this topic. The worker's job is to execute that answer, not re-derive it.

The load-bearing distinction under all five: **surfacing a direction (a "see") is not selecting it (a "decide").** Surfacing puts a direction in front of the selector; it is not the selection.

### Q3 — What did my prior finding get wrong, and how bounded is it?
My prior evaluation (the 00-21 finding) wrote: *"the selection job belongs to push — a watcher that surfaces the high-value aging directions uninvited"* / *"let push do the hard steering/selection job."* That **mislocated** steering on two counts:
- **See vs decide.** Surfacing aging directions is an *enumeration* (a "see"); selection is a *decide*. I called a see-function "the selection job." Push can *feed* selection; it is not selection.
- **Layer.** I framed pull and push as "two reads over the index" — reads a worker performs. But selection is a *cross-inquiry* decision, *above* the inquiry boundary. A worker-loop read is the wrong layer to own it.

**How bounded (non-sycophancy both ways):** the prior finding's *diagnosis* holds — pull's "feeds the steering layer" claim does over-reach, and the index is the valuable substrate. What's wrong is only the *prescription* ("add push as a second worker-loop read to steer"). So this corrects one recommendation, not the whole finding — and it does not make pull or the index "bad."

### Q4 — Where do pull and push actually land? The index feeds two layers.
Once selection is above the inquiry boundary, the index's consumers split cleanly by **layer**:
- **Below the boundary (the worker loop): pull.** A worker, at the start of its already-assigned inquiry, queries the index and enriches its chosen topic. Correctly in-layer. Its inability to answer "what should I work on next" is **not a defect** — that isn't its layer's job.
- **Above the boundary (navigation + orchestrator): the "push" function — already provided.** The navigational session, running over finished work, enumerates the whole field *including* the aging directions parked in the index; the orchestrator then selects using traversal memory. So "surface aging directions uninvited over the whole field" is **not a new worker-loop mechanism** — it is simply **making the open-directions index one of the inputs the eyes enumerate.** The organ exists; the index feeds it.

## Piece-Level Inversions

- **Q3 inverted — "my prescription was right; push does steer."** Tested: fails on both counts that killed it originally. Surfacing (push) is a *see*, and the canon reserves *decide/select* for the orchestrator — so a push-watcher, wherever it lives, feeds selection but doesn't perform it. And a worker-loop read can't own an above-boundary cross-inquiry decision. **KILLED; confirms the two-count mislocation.**
- **Q2 inverted — "the worker could/should steer the next move."** Tested: fails — the worker's control scope is per-cycle-only by canon, it lacks whole-field visibility, and by dispatch-order its topic was already chosen upstream. A worker that steered would import its tunnel-vision into the next move, defeating the isolation the architecture built for exactly this. **KILLED; confirms selection is above the worker.**

## Assembly Check — Emergents

- **E1 · The privileged-layer pattern** [HIGH; converged by Domain-transfer + Extrapolation + the canon]. Next-move-selection requires two things a worker structurally lacks: whole-field visibility and freedom from the finishing worker's tunnel-vision. That's *why* it's a separate privileged layer — the same reason an OS scheduler (not a worker process) picks the next process, and triage (not the surgeon mid-operation) picks the next patient. The layer separation isn't bureaucracy; it's what makes good selection possible.
- **E2 · "Push" was a mis-layered eyes-function** [HIGH; from Absence-recognition + Lens-shifting]. The cleanest statement of the correction: the "watcher that surfaces aging directions over the whole field" I called "push" is the *navigational session's* job (the eyes), which the architecture already has. I pushed an above-boundary eyes-function down into the worker-read layer. The fix isn't to build push — it's to feed the index to the eyes.
- **E3 · The error is a mis-layering, not a mistake of substance** [MED-HIGH]. My prior finding had the right *pieces* — the index is valuable, pull only enriches, and *something* must actively harvest the whole field. It just assigned the active-harvest to the wrong layer (a worker read) instead of the eyes. Calling it "mis-layering" both credits the substance and localizes the fault precisely.
