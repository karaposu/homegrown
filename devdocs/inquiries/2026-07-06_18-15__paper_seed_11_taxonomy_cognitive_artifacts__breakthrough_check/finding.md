# Finding: Paper 11 (Heersmink 2013, A Taxonomy of Cognitive Artifacts) — breakthrough or not?

## Question

The literal ask: *"dive deep into `devdocs/paper_seed/11.md` — breakthrough or not?"*

This is the latest dive in an ongoing **paper-harvest** — a series that takes one academic paper at a time and asks a single disciplined question: *does this paper hand us a genuinely new way to think or work (a "breakthrough"), or does it only confirm and sharpen what our project already has?* The harvest judges every paper by one fixed gate, the **import test**: an insight counts as a real import only if it either (a) **NAMES** something we already do but had never named, or (b) **RESOLVES** a confusion that genuinely pre-existed. Anything else is **confirming** (it agrees with us without adding). A "breakthrough" specifically means a **new frame or a new practice** — not the sharpening or relabeling of an existing one.

Paper 11 is Richard Heersmink (2013), "A Taxonomy of Cognitive Artifacts: Function, Information, and Categories" (*Review of Philosophy and Psychology*). It is a philosophy-of-technology paper that builds a **classification scheme** for *cognitive artifacts* — Heersmink's term for "human-made, physical objects that functionally contribute to performing a cognitive task" (a map, a notebook, a calculator, an abacus). It is the **fourth cognitive-artifacts paper in a row** in this harvest (after paper 14 on the extended mind, paper 13 on distributed cognition in hospital work, and paper 12 on Lao kinship gestures) — and it is by the **same author as paper 14** (this 2013 taxonomy is the earlier classificatory groundwork under his later 2015 extended-mind paper).

The goal: an import-test verdict, with any transferable substrate mapped into our own frame and the non-transferable physical/perceptual content set aside — neither manufacturing a breakthrough because the topic is on-point, nor reflexively dismissing the paper because it is "just a taxonomy" in a philosophy journal.

## Finding Summary

- **Verdict: NOT a breakthrough.** Paper 11 is **NO — confirming-plus at the LOW end.** Together with paper 12 it is one of the **two weakest dives in the recent harvest**, and marginally the thinner of the two on its distinctive (unique-to-this-paper) contribution.

- **The taxonomy, applied to us, collapses to a single cell.** Heersmink sorts cognitive artifacts into two big families — **representational** (they carry information *about* the world) and **ecological** (their physical arrangement carries information *as* the world) — and further into species (representational: iconic / indexical / symbolic; ecological: spatial / structural). When we sort *our own* artifacts (findings, canon documents, the route-index, the memory index, the file tree) into this scheme, **every one lands in the same box: representational-symbolic** (text whose meaning comes from convention). So the taxonomy does not organize a rich, multi-category picture of our work — it lands us in one corner of Heersmink's space.

- **That makes it a mirror, not an organizing tool — and a weak mirror.** Because our artifacts all fall in one cell, the taxonomy's value is a *contrast*: it clarifies, by showing what we lack, that we are a purely **symbolic-text** system, without the iconic (diagram-like), indexical (gauge-like), or ecological (spatial) richness that physical cognitive artifacts have. But this contrast is close to obvious (a text-only system produces text artifacts), and — unlike paper 12's mirror — it does not re-state any load-bearing commitment from our canon. So it is a *real but thin* mirror, weaker than paper 12's.

- **Its one useful edge just re-confirms paper 12.** The single place the taxonomy earns a little credit: it gives paper 12's leftover design-lesson a name. Paper 12 noted our artifacts are mostly *sentential* (linear prose) and that a *diagrammatic* form eases relational problems; Heersmink's taxonomy calls the diagrammatic form **iconic** and shows it is a species we under-use. This re-confirms and labels an existing observation — it changes no decision, so it folds into a category of confirming-plus we already have and mints nothing new.

- **A verification note worth recording.** For the first time in three dives, the harvest's canon-checking guard found **no over-statement** to correct — because the check was run *earlier* this time (during evidence-gathering, not only at the final critique), which prevented an over-claim from ever entering the reasoning. This validates a process change made after the previous dive.

## Finding

### The frame this verdict operates under

Two opposite errors shadow this dive. One is **manufacturing** an import: this is the fourth cognitive-artifacts paper in a row, it is *about* classifying cognitive artifacts (and we are a cognitive-artifact system), and "a taxonomy of our own artifacts" is a seductive framing — the pull to find "one more import" is real. The other is **reflexively dismissing** the paper because it is a philosophy-of-technology taxonomy, or because it is the same author's earlier and lesser-known work. The import test cuts through both: it asks only whether the paper names something we hadn't named or resolves a real confusion. The verdict below was earned against both pulls.

### The crux: the taxonomy collapses our artifact-space to one cell

Heersmink's classification has two top-level families:

- **Representational** artifacts carry information *about* the world (a map represents terrain). Within these, following the philosopher Peirce, he distinguishes three species: **iconic** (the artifact resembles/is structurally similar to its object — maps, diagrams, graphs), **indexical** (the artifact is causally connected to its object — a thermometer, a compass), and **symbolic** (the artifact means its object purely by convention — words, numerals, and, in his own words, "tables").
- **Ecological** artifacts carry information *as* the world: their physical arrangement itself does the cognitive work, without representing anything. His examples are car keys left on a particular spot, washed dishes placed in a "clean" zone, or Scrabble tiles rearranged until a word suggests itself. He is explicit that these have "no representational content."

Now sort *our* artifacts into this. Findings, canon documents, the route-index, the memory index, the state and branch files, the discipline outputs — all are **text whose meaning comes from convention**, which is Heersmink's definition of **symbolic**. There are no indexical artifacts (nothing we have is causally wired to its referent the way a thermometer is). And — this is the load-bearing point — there are **no genuinely ecological ones either**.

The tempting objection (and the strongest case for a richer reading) is that our *organization* is ecological: a file's **location** encodes its status (a file in the `docarchive/` folder means "concluded"), the done/explored check-marks in the route-index encode progress, the file tree encodes structure. Isn't location-carries-information exactly Heersmink's "ecological-spatial"?

It is not — and the reason is a **mechanism**, not a preference. Heersmink's ecological artifacts carry information by a *perceived physical arrangement* (you *see* the keys on the spot) or by *manipulation* (rearranging the tiles makes new information emerge), and crucially they are **non-representational**. Our organizational cues are the opposite: a directory named `docarchive` means "concluded" by a **convention** (we decided the word means that); a check-mark means "done" by convention; the route-index is a **table**, which Heersmink himself classifies as "predominantly symbolic." We do not *perceive* a spatial layout — we *read path strings and tables*, which mean what they mean by agreement. That is symbolic, not ecological. Pushing the check one level deeper: our system has **no** information-bearing structure that works without convention — it is symbolic all the way down.

So the taxonomy does not sort our work into many categories; it places all of it in one. The consequence for the verdict is direct: an external classification scheme can only be a genuine **organizing import** (the way paper 13's usability criteria organized our artifact-quality commitments) if it sorts a *differentiated* space. Ours is not differentiated — it is monochromatic — so the taxonomy **organizes nothing**. Its only remaining function is the contrast: the mirror.

### Why the mirror is weak

The mirror says: *our artifact-space is monochromatically symbolic, lacking the iconic, indexical, and ecological richness of physical cognitive artifacts.* Two things keep this thin. First, it is nearly self-evident — a symbolic-text system will of course produce symbolic artifacts; the contrast does not surprise. Second, and more decisively for the harvest, it does **not re-state a load-bearing commitment** of ours. (Paper 12's mirror did: its "the anchor is not even necessary" claim was the exact inverse of our canon rule that *external memory is inert until loaded*, so it vividly re-instanced something we depend on. Paper 11 has no such anchor to re-instance.) The mirror is real — it is not nothing — but it sits at the low end.

The one edge that gives it any bite is the connection to paper 12. Paper 12 left a thin design-lesson: our findings are mostly **sentential** (linear prose), and where a problem is genuinely relational a **diagrammatic** form (a table, a tree, the route-index) makes the relationships easier to read off. Heersmink's taxonomy gives that lesson a vocabulary: the diagrammatic form is **iconic**, and iconic (along with ecological) is precisely the species we under-use. This is a genuine but small contribution — it *re-confirms and names* an existing observation. By the harvest's test for whether a naming is worth minting a new category (does it change any decision?), it does not: we already use tables and the route-index where relationships matter. So it folds into a confirming-plus category we already keep, and adds no new one.

### The substrate maps in as plain confirmation

The rest of the paper's transferable ideas confirm things we already hold:

- **Exograms vs engrams** (Heersmink, via Merlin Donald): external representations have different properties from biological memory. This confirms our standing distinction between external files and the small live working context — **but with a caveat that matters for honesty.** Our canon frames this distinction around **cost** ("our memory is external, so retrieval costs a load; files are inert until loaded"). Heersmink's angle is *capacity-complement* (external media exceed biological memory in size and are more malleable). That capacity angle is **not in our canon**, and it does not transfer anyway — we are not a small-biological-store system that external media enlarge; our memory is *already* external. So this is confirming on the part that maps, and non-transferable on the part that doesn't. It is not a new import.
- **The functional-kind definition** (an artifact is a cognitive artifact by virtue of what it *does*, not its intrinsic form or its designer's intent) rhymes with our sense that our artifacts are defined by their function (a finding is a finding because it records a decision). Confirming, and thin.
- **The artifact-centered method** (classify an artifact by its *own* properties, not by the cognitive process it serves) rhymes with our canon principle that a discipline's specification must describe what the discipline *is* without naming the upstream or downstream disciplines that consume its output. A genuine cross-domain rhyme — but we already hold the principle richly, so it confirms rather than imports.

### What does not transfer (the quarantine)

Applying the harvest's **reverse-lens** — separating what is true of physical cognitive artifacts from what transfers to our external-file, symbolic-text architecture — produces the **largest quarantine in the harvest so far**. Non-transferable: the entire ecological family (car keys, dishes, Scrabble, Tetris — all perceptual/spatial/manipulable); the iconic and indexical species (resemblance and causal-connection to a *physical* world, which a text system barely instantiates); the naturefact category (natural objects used for cognition, e.g. stars for navigation — we have no natural objects); and "the world is its own best model" (using a perceived environment as an external stand-in — we have no perceived environment). This is most of the paper.

### Where this sits in the harvest

This is the **tenth consecutive paper** whose transferable residue maps into our memory frame — **grasp-management** (the frame that memory lives in external files; that the fraction a session holds live is limited; that looking something up is not the same as holding it) — without breaching it. Here it maps in almost entirely as confirmation and contrast, not fit.

## Reasoning

The verdict is **NO / confirming-plus, LOW — one of the two weakest recent dives**, and it was stress-tested rather than assumed.

- **The it-IS-a-breakthrough inversion** ("the taxonomy classifies our artifact-space, or the exogram/engram capacity idea is the unnamed import") was **rejected.** Our artifact-space collapses to one cell, so there is nothing rich to classify; and the capacity-complement idea quarantines (we are not a small-biological-store system) while its transferable core is already ours.

- **The organizing-import inversion** ("our check-marks, file tree, and route-index are ecological, so the taxonomy sorts a rich space") was **defeated on the mechanism, not on precedent.** Heersmink's ecological category is defined by non-representational, perceived-spatial or manipulation-based information; our organizational cues carry meaning by convention, which is his own definition of symbolic (he classifies tables as "predominantly symbolic"). Even granting the surface resemblance, our artifacts are symbolic by the taxonomy's own criterion. This is where the dive did its hardest work, and it sharpened the verdict rather than softening it.

- **What survived:** the NO verdict; the collapse-to-one-cell finding; the weak mirror; the thin, folded iconic/diagrammatic re-confirmation; the large, precise quarantine; and three provisional method-observations (the differentiated-target-space refinement, the category-set-freeze evidence, and the guard's earlier-application success). No candidate for a genuine new frame or practice survived, because none was there.

- **The one correction from the adversarial pass** was in the *accounting*, not the verdict: the "differentiated-target-space" refinement was initially framed as resting on four data points, but the *added condition* specifically rests on one (paper 11). The finding carries both counts and holds the refinement provisional.

- **The honesty note, in the credit direction:** the harvest's guard against over-claiming canon coverage found no over-statement this dive — the first clean run in three — because it was applied earlier in the process. That is the process change from the previous dive working as intended.
