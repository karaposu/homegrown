## PART A — The Paper's Insights (FACTs, quoted where load-bearing)

- **A-1 — WORKING MEMORY IS RICH-BUT-POOR [core].** *Rich in access:* working memory *"can draw information from sensory channels (vision, touch, smell etc), as well as from other memory systems such as long-term memory and also the brain's system for processing language"* — it is *"where a lot of the information in your brain comes together."* *Rich in processing:* it contains sub-systems — visual/spatial reasoning, chunk-storage, and *"a 'central executive' system… like a merciless boss, assigning tasks to the different systems within working memory and keeping everything under control."* *Poor in capacity:* despite this, *"working memory can only actually store a tiny amount of information at any one time."* The romance the author names: *"it can always see the vast riches available to it, but can only ever sample a tiny portion at a time."*

- **A-2 — THE DOORWAY EFFECT [core].** Entering a new room flushes working memory: *"when we enter a new room, its like the brain flushes away the old facts from working memory, to get ready for the fresh information that we might need in our new setting."* The cause is low capacity: *"There's not much room in working memory, so when new information comes in, old information needs to go."* It is functional: *"forgetting old information is important in helping us to stay open and alert to novel information in the new environment."* [supporting: the evolutionary framing]

- **A-3 — CAPACITY: SLOTS vs FLEXIBLE-RESOURCE [supporting].** The slots model: *"the capacity of working memory is only about four 'slots'. Once those slots are taken, working memory is full up."* The rival flexible-resource model: capacity *"is more of a flexible resource that can be differently distributed across different pieces of information… like a tank of water… you can give a little bit of water to lots of different areas, or lots of water to just one or two areas."* Crucially, **capacity scales with complexity**: for *"a very complicated object (like a cube with many different coloured sides) [capacity] might only be between 1 and 2"* — *"its capacity depends on how complicated the information you're trying to store is."*

- **A-4 — CHUNKING [supporting].** Familiar patterns compress: `BBC FBI WWF` (familiar → 3 chunks, 3 slots) vs `ZQK EWP WLJ` (unfamiliar → 9 individual chunks). *"they're familiar, and so naturally your brain sorts them into three chunks."* Chunking is how the same capacity holds more when the content is patterned.

- **A-5 — WORKING MEMORY ↔ CONSCIOUSNESS (the global workspace) [core].** *"the global neuronal workspace theory… suggests that consciousness arises as a result of information being 'broadcast' in a 'global workspace'… like a central information store, which can process information and distribute it globally to many different systems."* And: *"the global workspace and working memory are similar notions."* Attention is the switch: *"when we pay attention to information that is stored in working memory, that information gets boosted in volume and is broadcast across the brain – and that is what makes it conscious… consciousness arises when working memory and attention work together."* The slogan: **no attention = no consciousness** (the phone-distracted people: *"75% of people who were on their mobile phones completely failed to spot a purple and yellow clown unicycling around the courtyard"*).

- **A-6 — THE REFRIGERATOR-LIGHT ILLUSION [core — the standout].** We feel conscious of a whole rich scene, but *"really you're only ever conscious of a few scraps at any one time. The reason it feels like you're conscious of more is because, whenever you pay attention to something, that attention boosts the information into consciousness."* Named: *"the refrigerator light illusion. Imagine someone who thought the light in their fridge was always turned on, because whenever they open the door to check, the light is on… the very act of opening the door causes the light to come on."* Conclusion: *"we are only ever conscious of a few little bits at a time, but the ease with which attention can make things conscious fosters the illusion that we're conscious of a lot more."* (Its trigger is the *overflow* problem: *"consciousness overflows the capacity of working memory."*)

- **A-7 — WORKING-MEMORY-WITHOUT-CONSCIOUSNESS (the Gabor patch) [supporting].** A patch flashed for *"only 16.67 milliseconds"* is not consciously seen, yet participants judged a second patch's tilt relative to it *"at a level above chance."* Conclusion: *"information about the briefly flashed patch must be stored in working memory, even though participants were completely unaware of it… some information in working memory is not conscious."* So working memory and consciousness are not identical.

- **A-8 — THE AUTHOR'S THESIS: WORKING MEMORY IS A SPECTRUM [core].** *"working memory might not be an all-or-nothing thing… information might not have to be either 'in' or 'out' of working memory."* Instead: *"working memory comes on a spectrum. Some information is definitely in working memory, and some is definitely not. But in between… is a large grey area."* The briefly-flashed patch *"sits in the grey area between being fully in and fully out of working memory."*

- **C-H3 — Doorway-flush ↔ compaction / fresh-perception between steps [supporting].** Entering a new discipline (or a new traverse) flushes the prior live context; the isolated navigation session's "fresh perception each time" is a deliberate doorway. We already do the flush. [inference]
- **C-H4 — Chunking ↔ compact reference [supporting].** A well-named concept or a canon-doc citation is a "chunk" — it lets the same context hold more by referring to a pattern instead of spelling it out. We already rely on this (findings cite canon rather than restating it). [inference]
- **C-H5 — Attention-broadcast ↔ what's loaded is what's operative [supporting].** What a session loads into context is what it can act on; loading is our "attention." We already live this constraint. [inference]

### LACK — insights that name something we plausibly need but have no concept for
- **C-L1 — THE REFRIGERATOR-LIGHT ILLUSION as a named failure mode [core — the standout candidate].** A session *feels* it "has" all the canon and memory because it can look anything up on demand — but at any moment only the **loaded fraction is live**. The act of checking (looking something up) makes it present, fostering the illusion it was operative all along. We have no name for this. It bears directly on cold-navigation (B-4) and on *why* pre-loading/warming matters: you cannot rely on "it's lookup-able"; only what's loaded is actually working. Likely the second elegant import. [inference — for sensemaking to adjudicate against the import test]
- **C-L2 — Capacity-scales-with-COMPLEXITY [core/supporting].** A dense, complex memory item (a raw inquiry finding) soaks up far more context than a simple one (a one-line gist) — the paper's "complex object → capacity 1-2." We treat context as roughly uniform; we have no concept that complex items cost more to hold. This *explains the payoff of consolidation* (B-2): gist is cheaper to hold than raw episode. Cross-paper link. [inference]
- **C-L3 — The IN/OUT SPECTRUM (partial presence) [supporting].** Context can be *partially* present: a fact degraded inside a summary (mentioned, not fully loaded), or fading in a compaction. We treat context as binary (in-context or not). The "large grey area" names partial presence — a real state we have no vocabulary for. [inference]
- **C-L4 — The rich-but-poor GAP as the design pressure [supporting].** The gap between what's *reachable* (vast) and what's *holdable* (tiny) is exactly the pressure that makes memory design necessary — you cannot hold it all, so you must choose what to load. It names *why* warming, indexing, and consolidation exist at all. [inference]

The consciousness claims don't transfer literally. The *functional* residue (what's "broadcast"/operative = what's loaded-and-attended) DOES map (see C-H5); flagged partial, not a clean transfer. - **C-D3 — The doorway effect's evolutionary *why* [peripheral].** *"forgetting… evolved to stay alert to novelty"* — the reason-it-evolved doesn't transfer; the functional shape (flush-on-context-change) does (C-H3).
- **C-D4 — Working-memory-WITHOUT-consciousness (the Gabor patch) [supporting — partial].** Our system has no consciousness, so "unconscious working memory" has no literal analog. The *functional* residue: information can be present-in-context without being *foregrounded* — like text in a loaded file the session hasn't focused on. That partial map (present-but-not-attended) is real; the consciousness framing is not. [inference, flagged partial]

### REVERSE — what WE have that the paper doesn't cover
- **C-R1 — Our flush is DELIBERATE and DESIGNED [core — inference].** The doorway effect is involuntary and accidental in humans; our compaction and fresh-perception are *chosen*. We decide what survives the doorway (what a summary carries forward). Humans cannot. We have *controllable* working memory the paper's subject lacks.
- **C-R2 — EXTERNALIZED working memory [core — inference, resolves a prior open question].** Our working memory can be *written out* (a scratchpad file, `_state.md`) and *re-loaded* — persisted across the "doorway." Human working memory cannot be externalized; it just vanishes. **This is exactly why our working memory is not purely transient** — it can be dumped to disk and restored. This bears directly on the prior finding's flagged category-error (B-1): the question "does transient working memory need an artifact?" has an answer the paper's subject can't have — *it can*, and that externalizability is what distinguishes ours.
